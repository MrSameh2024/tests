Passage: The Return of Urban Wetlands

A For much of the twentieth century, wetlands within cities were often viewed as wasted land. Marshes and shallow ponds were drained to make space for roads, housing and industry. More recently, however, urban planners have begun to reconsider this approach. Wetlands are now increasingly recognised as useful infrastructure that can support both environmental and social goals.

B One important benefit is flood management. Wetlands can temporarily hold rainwater after heavy storms, reducing the speed at which water reaches drains and rivers. This does not mean that wetlands can prevent every flood. Their effectiveness depends on their size, location and the amount of water entering the system. Nevertheless, when combined with other measures, they can reduce pressure on urban drainage networks.

C Wetlands may also improve biodiversity. Even small areas of shallow water and vegetation can provide feeding or breeding places for insects, amphibians and birds. In some cities, restoration projects have encouraged species that had disappeared from heavily developed neighbourhoods. Yet these gains are not automatic. If water quality is poor or invasive plants dominate, the ecological value of a site may remain limited.

D A further attraction is that wetlands can become public spaces. Paths, viewing platforms and educational signs can turn them into places for walking and outdoor learning. This social role can help local residents understand why the site is being protected. On the other hand, planners must balance public access with habitat protection. Too much disturbance can reduce the value of the wetland for wildlife.

E Successful projects therefore require long-term management. Restoring a wetland is not a one-time construction task. Water levels, vegetation, litter and public use may all need to be monitored. The most effective schemes are usually those in which engineers, ecologists and local communities share responsibility rather than treating the wetland as the concern of only one professional group.

Questions 1–8

1. In the past, many city wetlands were removed to create space for development.

Answer: True.
Paragraph A says wetlands were drained to make space for roads, housing and industry.
Strategy: Match the statement to a clear factual claim in the passage.
Vocabulary: drained = water removed; development = building/use of land.

2. Urban wetlands are able to stop all flooding during severe storms.

Answer: False.
Paragraph B explicitly says wetlands cannot prevent every flood.
Strategy: Watch for absolute words such as “all” and “every.”

3. Restored wetlands always increase nearby property prices.

Answer: Not Given.
The passage does not discuss property prices.
Strategy: Do not use outside knowledge. If the passage gives no evidence for or against the claim, choose Not Given.

4. Which paragraph explains how wetlands can reduce pressure on city drainage systems?

Answer: B.
Paragraph B explains temporary water storage and reduced pressure on drainage networks.
Strategy: Match ideas, not repeated words.

5. Which paragraph warns that ecological benefits may be limited by poor conditions?

Answer: C.
Paragraph C mentions poor water quality and invasive plants limiting ecological value.
Vocabulary: invasive = spreading in a harmful/uncontrolled way.

6. Complete the sentence using words from the passage.
Planners need to balance public access with __________.

Answer: habitat protection.
Paragraph D: “planners must balance public access with habitat protection.”
Strategy: Respect the wording and spelling from the passage.

7. Complete the sentence using words from the passage.
Successful wetland projects require __________.

Answer: long-term management.
Paragraph E begins: “Successful projects therefore require long-term management.”
Vocabulary: long-term = continuing over an extended period.

8. Who should share responsibility in the most effective wetland schemes?

Answer: Engineers, ecologists and local communities.
Paragraph E identifies all three groups.
Strategy: For “who” questions, scan for groups or people, then verify the full sentence.
