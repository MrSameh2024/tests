Part B — Reading Diagnostic

Passage: Shared Workspaces and Productivity

In recent years, shared workspaces have become common in many cities. Originally associated mainly with freelancers and small start-up companies, they are now also used by larger organisations seeking flexible office arrangements. Supporters argue that shared spaces can reduce costs and encourage interaction between people from different professional backgrounds.

However, research into productivity presents a mixed picture. Some workers report that informal conversations lead to useful ideas and new collaborations. Others find the same environment distracting, particularly when they need to complete tasks that require sustained concentration. Noise levels, seating design and access to private rooms can therefore influence whether a shared workspace supports or reduces productivity.

Another factor is individual preference. Employees who enjoy frequent social contact may adapt quickly, while those who prefer quiet and predictable surroundings may need more control over where and when they work. For this reason, some organisations now combine shared areas with bookable quiet rooms and remote-working options rather than adopting a single office model for everyone.

Overall, shared workspaces are neither universally beneficial nor universally harmful. Their success depends on how well the physical environment, job requirements and individual needs are matched. Organisations that simply copy fashionable office designs without considering these factors may gain flexibility but fail to improve actual performance.
